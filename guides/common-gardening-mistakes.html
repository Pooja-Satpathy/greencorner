10 Common Gardening Mistakes (and How to Fix Them)

Every gardener loses a plant or two — it's how we learn. These are the slip-ups we see most often, and the simple habits that prevent them.

GCBy GreenCorner Editorial TeamPublished 2 November 2026 · Updated 7 November 20267 min read

1. Overwatering

More houseplants die from too much water than too little. Constantly wet soil starves roots of oxygen, leading to root rot, yellowing leaves and fungus gnats.

Fix: water only when the top few centimetres of soil are dry, and water less in winter and during the monsoon. Our guide on how often to water indoor plants shows you how to check.

2. Using pots without drainage holes

Without a hole, excess water collects at the bottom of the pot where you can't see it. A layer of pebbles at the bottom doesn't solve this — water still pools in the soil above the stones.

Fix: always plant in pots with drainage holes, such as terracotta pots. If you love a pot without a hole, use it as a decorative cover around a nursery pot and empty it after watering.

3. Choosing the spot before the plant

It's tempting to buy a plant for an empty corner, but if that corner is dark, a sun-loving plant will stretch, fade and decline.

Fix: match the plant to the light. Low-light corners suit snake plants and ZZ plants; flowering plants such as hibiscus need 5–6 hours of direct sun. The Plant Finder does the matching for you.

4. Filling pots with garden soil alone

Garden soil compacts in containers, drains poorly and can bring in pests or weed seeds.

Fix: use a light potting mix, or blend garden soil with cocopeat and compost so the mix stays airy.

5. Overfeeding with fertiliser

More fertiliser doesn't mean more growth. Excess salts can burn roots, causing brown leaf tips and a white crust on the soil.

Fix: feed diluted, and only while plants are actively growing. Never feed a bone-dry plant — water first — and flush the soil with plain water every month or two.

6. Repotting too soon or into a pot that's too big

A much larger pot holds a lot of soil that roots can't reach, so it stays wet for too long. Repotting a stressed new plant immediately adds to the shock.

Fix: let new plants settle for a week or two, then move up only one pot size (2–5 cm wider) when roots fill the current pot — ideally while the plant is actively growing.

7. Moving plants into strong sun suddenly

Plants grown in a nursery's shade or indoors can scorch when moved straight into harsh sun, leaving bleached or brown patches.

Fix: acclimatise gradually over one to two weeks — start with gentle morning sun and increase exposure a little each day.

8. Ignoring pests until it's too late

Mealybugs, aphids, spider mites and scale insects multiply quickly and often hide under leaves.

Fix: check leaf undersides when you water. Keep new plants apart for a week or two. Remove small infestations with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol. Neem-based sprays are a popular option — follow the label and test on a few leaves first.

9. Watering every plant on the same schedule

A succulent and a peace lily on the same "every Sunday" routine won't both be happy.

Fix: group plants by their needs and check the soil of each. The free Plant Care Planner lets you set a different watering interval for every plant.

10. Buying too many plants at once

A sudden jungle is hard to keep up with, and problems spread easily between crowded plants.

Fix: start with two or three easy plants from our list of the best indoor plants for beginners, then add more as you learn what thrives in your home.

A quick plant-health checklist

  • Every pot has a drainage hole, and saucers are emptied after watering.
  • You check the soil before watering — not the calendar.
  • Each plant is in light that suits it.
  • Leaf undersides are checked for pests every week or two.
  • Feeding happens only during the growing season.
  • Tools are clean: wipe pruning shears with rubbing alcohol between plants and wear gardening gloves for thorny or sappy plants.
